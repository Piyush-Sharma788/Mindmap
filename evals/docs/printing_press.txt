Around 1440, Johannes Gutenberg developed a printing press with movable metal type in Mainz, Germany. Earlier printing in Europe used carved wooden blocks, one per page, which was slow and expensive. Gutenberg's key inventions were a hand mould for casting identical metal letters quickly, an oil-based ink that stuck to metal, and a screw press adapted from wine making.

The Gutenberg Bible, finished around 1455, showed that printed books could match the quality of hand-copied manuscripts. Within fifty years, printing shops had spread to more than two hundred European cities and had produced millions of books.

Cheaper books changed who could read and what they read. Literacy rose, and texts in local languages became common instead of Latin only. Printing let scholars compare identical copies of a text, which helped standardise spelling and supported the Scientific Revolution, because results could be shared and checked widely.

Printing also had political and religious effects. Martin Luther's pamphlets of 1517 spread through Germany in weeks, fuelling the Protestant Reformation. Authorities responded with censorship and licensing of printers, an early fight over the control of information.
